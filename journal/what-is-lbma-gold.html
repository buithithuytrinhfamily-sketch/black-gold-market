A standard and a list of refiners, not a grade of metal

What Is LBMA Gold

The label does not describe purer metal. It describes a bar from an accredited refiner, and the LBMA's own vault figures let you check the specification with one division.

A dealer calls its product investment grade, a vaulting service calls its metal institutional, and somewhere in the small print the four letters appear. So what is LBMA gold, and is the label something you can actually check? It is, and the thing it names is not a grade of metal. It is a standard, a published list of refiners, and a set of hurdles a refinery has to clear before the London market will take its bars without weighing and assaying every single one.

This sits under Master rather than Protect, but only just. The expensive version of this question is not asked by someone reading about vaults. It is asked by someone who has already sent money to a dealer, or who has opened a position in a product whose name contains the word gold, and who is working out afterwards what they are actually holding. The cheap version is the one you are reading now.

What Is LBMA Gold, the Short Answer

LBMA stands for the London Bullion Market Association. It does not mine gold, it does not refine gold, and it does not sell gold. What it does own and manage are the Good Delivery Lists, which the association describes as lists of gold and silver refiners who are accredited by the LBMA to supply gold and silver bars into the London market.

So when somebody says LBMA gold, they are using a loose shorthand for something fairly precise: a bar produced by a refiner on that list, made to the Good Delivery specification. The phrase describes provenance and form, not a secret purity tier.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A bar is not better metal because of the stamp on it. Gold is an element. What the standard buys is acceptance without argument. A Good Delivery bar moves between counterparties in London without each one re-assaying it, because the refiner that made it has already been checked and keeps being checked.

The Good Delivery List, and What a Refiner Has to Prove

Getting on the list is not a form and a fee. The LBMA sets out hurdles on scale, on financial strength, and on the bars themselves.

On scale, a refiner applying for the gold list has to produce at least 10 tonnes of refined gold a year. For silver the figure is 50 tonnes. On financial strength, the applicant has to show tangible net worth of at least 15 million pounds. On the bars, the association's requirements cover purity and physical appearance, including marking and surface quality.

Read those three hurdles together and the design becomes obvious. Two of them are not about metal at all. The output test and the net worth test are about whether the refiner will still be there in ten years, and whether it is large enough that its reputation is worth more to it than any single dishonest bar could ever be.

That is the quiet engineering of the whole thing. A standard that only tested the metal in front of you would have to be re-run on every bar forever. A standard that tests the institution behind the metal can be run once and then maintained.

Weighing the London Vaults, a Number You Can Check Yourself

Here is the part most explanations skip, and it is the part that turns a claim into something you can audit at your own desk.

The LBMA publishes what is sitting in the London vaults. At the end of September 2026 the reported holding was 9,821 tonnes of gold, held as approximately 785,690 bars, with a reported value of around 1.3 trillion dollars, up 1.96 per cent on the previous month.

Two of those numbers are enough to test the bar standard without taking anybody's word for it. Divide the tonnage by the bar count and you get the average bar.

The assumptions, written out so you can disagree with them: one tonne is 1,000,000 grams, one troy ounce is 31.1034768 grams, and the division treats the vaulted holding as bars of one broad type, which is how the LBMA reports it.

Doing it: 9,821 tonnes is 9,821,000,000 grams. Divided by 785,690 bars that is 12,499.8 grams per bar, or 12.500 kilograms. Converted to troy ounces, 401.88.

Now compare that with what the LBMA says a Good Delivery gold bar nominally is, which is approximately 400 troy ounces. The vault arithmetic lands within about half a per cent of the published nominal size. Nobody had to be trusted for that. It is two published figures and one division, and anybody reading this can repeat it.

That is the healthiest way to hold any market fact. Not because the LBMA is suspected of anything, but because a number you have checked behaves differently in your head than a number you have been handed.

Why the Standard Exists at All

Imagine the market without it. Every bar arriving at every counterparty would need to be weighed and assayed, because the cost of being wrong once is larger than the cost of checking every time. Settlement would slow to the speed of a laboratory. Price would carry a permanent discount for doubt.

The Good Delivery standard replaces that with fungibility. One accredited bar is as good as another accredited bar, so bars become interchangeable units rather than individual objects with individual histories. Trade can then happen at the speed of a phone call, and the metal itself can sit still.

This is also why London gold is quoted loco London, meaning the price refers to metal in an accepted London vault. Location and acceptability are part of what is being priced. Gold in a vault that the market will not accept is still gold, but it is not the same tradeable thing.

What Is LBMA Gold to a Retail Trader

Almost nobody reading this will ever stand next to a 12.5 kilogram bar, and that is fine. The reason to understand the standard is to be clear about what you are and are not holding.

If you trade gold as a contract for difference or as a spot position with a broker, you do not own a bar. You hold a contract whose price references this market. That is not a criticism of the instrument, it is simply what the instrument is, and the people who get hurt are usually the ones who thought it was something else.

If you buy into a vaulted product, the question worth asking is whether your holding is allocated, meaning specific identified bars are held for you, or unallocated, meaning you are a creditor of the institution with a claim measured in metal. Both arrangements are normal and both are used by serious institutions. They behave very differently if the institution itself gets into trouble, which is exactly the scenario the arrangement exists to survive.

So the practical use of knowing what the standard is: it lets you ask a better question than is this real gold. The better question is what exactly do I have a claim on, who owes it to me, and what happens to that claim if they fail.

What This Does Not Say

It does not say accreditation is a guarantee. It is a standard with hurdles and ongoing checks, which lowers the chance of a bad bar reaching the market. No standard anywhere reduces a risk to zero, and treating a list as a promise is how people stop reading the rest of the paperwork.

It does not say the vault figure is a measure of the world's gold. It is the reported holding in London vaults at one month end. Metal moves, and other vaults in other cities exist and publish separately or not at all.

It does not say anything about where any price is going. Nothing in this article is a view on direction, and nothing here should be read as a reason to buy or sell anything today.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is LBMA gold purer than ordinary gold?
Not as a category. Gold is an element, and a bar from an accredited refiner is not made of better atoms. The standard covers purity along with physical appearance, marking and surface quality, and what accreditation buys is acceptance without re-assay rather than a secret grade.

Does the LBMA sell gold?
No. It is a trade association. It owns and manages the Good Delivery Lists of accredited refiners, and it publishes market data including London vault holdings. It is not a dealer and it is not a vault operator.

How heavy is an LBMA gold bar?
The nominal Good Delivery gold bar is approximately 400 troy ounces. Working from the LBMA's own September 2026 vault figures, 9,821 tonnes across about 785,690 bars gives an average of 12.500 kilograms, or 401.88 troy ounces, which is within about half a per cent of that nominal.

Can I buy a Good Delivery bar?
In practice this is a wholesale object rather than a retail one. At roughly 12.5 kilograms a bar it is a large single unit, and it is designed to move between vaults and institutions rather than to sit in a house. Retail bars and coins are made to other specifications entirely.

Does my broker's gold price come from these bars?
Your broker quotes a price derived from this wholesale market, but a retail trading position is a contract with your broker, not a title to metal. If you want to know what stands behind your position, the document to read is your broker's terms, not the LBMA's.

Where did the numbers in this article come from?
The accreditation hurdles and the nominal bar size are from the LBMA's own Good Delivery pages. The vault figures are from the LBMA's published London vault data for the end of September 2026. The 12.500 kilogram average bar is not quoted anywhere, it is a division of those two published figures, with the assumptions written out above so you can repeat or reject it.
