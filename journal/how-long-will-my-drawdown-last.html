A date is what you want, arithmetic is what exists

How Long Will My Drawdown Last

Depth decides the size of the climb and your sustainable pace decides the speed. Down 20 per cent needs a 25.00 per cent gain, down 50 per cent needs a doubling.

By Raphael  ·  XAU/USD  ·  10 min read

The question never arrives calmly. It arrives on an evening when the equity curve has been pointing the wrong way for weeks, when the account is smaller than it was in the spring, and what you want is not another lecture about risk. What you want is a date. How long will my drawdown last is the honest form of that want, and it deserves an honest answer in two halves. One half is pure arithmetic and I can give it to you exactly. The other half depends on a number only you control, and anyone who hands you a confident date for it is selling something.

One reader wrote: "Yeah I am currently on phase 1 on my funded account and in drawdown." Another: "I did follow one of your signals two weeks ago, when there was a drawdown you updated the group to stop out, but I did not, I let it run. My ego and my shallow thinking cost me losing again and again." A third: "Sorry, I lost more than half of my capital. I got too greedy." A fourth: "I don't even know this rule, I get multiple positions, it doesn't touch the stop loss but it reached max position drawdown." About 29 messages in my inbox mention drawdown directly, which is a keyword count rather than a survey, and almost none of them ask what a drawdown is. They ask when it ends.

Why the question has two different answers

A drawdown has a depth and a duration, and people tend to feel the duration while the depth is what actually governs it. Depth is the distance from your highest account value to where you are now. Duration is how long it takes to get back to that high.

These are related but they are not the same measurement, and confusing them is why the answer so often feels evasive. The depth is already fixed, it happened, and it dictates the size of the climb. The duration is not fixed at all, because it depends on the pace you can sustain from here and on whether you dig any further down while you are climbing.

So the useful version of the question is not "when will this end". It is "how big is the climb, and what pace can I honestly sustain without making the hole deeper". Those two have answers.

How long will my drawdown last, the half that is pure arithmetic

The first thing to internalise is that a drawdown is not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is not psychological, it is mathematical. The gain required to get back to your old peak is the drawdown divided by one minus the drawdown, because you have to earn that gain on a balance smaller than the one that lost it.

Run it out. Down 10 per cent needs an 11.11 per cent gain. Down 20 per cent needs 25.00 per cent. Down 30 per cent needs 42.86 per cent. Down 40 per cent needs 66.67 per cent. Down 50 per cent needs a 100.00 per cent gain, meaning you must double what is left. Down 60 per cent needs 150.00 per cent.

Look at the shape rather than the individual rows. The first ten points of loss cost you eleven points to undo. The ten points between a 50 and a 60 per cent drawdown cost you fifty points to undo. The cost of each additional slice of depth accelerates, which is the entire mathematical argument for capping depth early rather than deciding to get serious later. The reader who said he lost more than half of his capital is, arithmetically, facing a doubling. Not a recovery, a doubling.

This is also why "I just need one good month" is usually false comfort. One good month undoes a shallow drawdown and barely dents a deep one.

The half that depends on your pace, not the market's mood

Now the duration. If you assume a steady compounding net return each month, the time to climb out is the logarithm of one over one minus the drawdown, divided by the logarithm of one plus that monthly return. I want to be explicit that this assumes something untrue, namely that results arrive smoothly and that you take no further losses. Real equity curves do not behave like that. Treat the output as a floor on the time, the best possible case, not a forecast.

At a net half a per cent a month, a 10 per cent drawdown takes about 21.1 months to recover, a 20 per cent takes about 44.7 months, a 30 per cent about 71.5 months, and a 50 per cent about 139.0 months. At one per cent a month those become about 10.6, 22.4, 35.8 and 69.7 months. At two per cent a month, about 5.3, 11.3, 18.0 and 35.0 months. At three per cent a month, about 3.6, 7.5, 12.1 and 23.4 months.

Sit with the 50 per cent row for a moment. Even at a sustained three per cent net every single month with no further losses, a halved account takes nearly two years to see its old high. That is the mathematical reality underneath the emotional one, and it explains why so many traders in deep drawdown quietly abandon the account and open a new one. They are not weak. They are looking at a climb that the arithmetic says is long, and no amount of determination shortens it.

The honest answer to how long it lasts, then, is: as long as the depth divided by your sustainable pace says it will, plus whatever extra time you add by making it deeper.

How the hole got dug, and why that sets the floor

There is a third number worth computing, because it connects the drawdown to the decision that caused it. If you risk a fixed fraction of equity per trade and lose repeatedly, the number of consecutive full losses needed to reach a given depth is the logarithm of one minus the drawdown divided by the logarithm of one minus the risk fraction.

Risking half a per cent per trade, it takes about 21 straight losses to be down 10 per cent, about 45 to be down 20, and about 71 to be down 30. At one per cent, about 10.5, 22 and 36. At two per cent, about 5, 11 and 18. At five per cent per trade, it takes only about 2 straight losses to be down 10 per cent, about 4 to be down 20, and about 7 to be down 30.

That last row is the whole lesson. At five per cent per trade, an utterly ordinary run of seven losses puts you in a hole that requires a 42.86 per cent gain to escape. The run was not unusual. The size was. This is the same point made from the other direction in position sizing so one trade cannot hurt you, and it is the single highest leverage change available to anyone reading this in drawdown right now.

Losing runs are ordinary, which is the part nobody budgets for

People treat a losing streak as evidence that something has broken. Usually it is evidence that they have been trading for a while. Assuming independent trades, which is an approximation because real losing runs cluster, the chance of hitting at least one streak of five straight losses somewhere in a hundred trades is about 81.0 per cent if each trade loses half the time. A streak of eight straight has about a 17.0 per cent chance, and ten straight about 4.4 per cent.

If your trades lose 60 per cent of the time, which is entirely normal for an approach that wins big and loses small, the chance of a five loss streak in a hundred trades rises to about 97.6 per cent, an eight loss streak to about 49.0 per cent, and a ten loss streak to about 20.5 per cent.

Read those numbers as a budgeting exercise rather than a warning. If an eight trade losing run is close to a coin flip across a hundred trades, then your risk per trade has to be small enough that an eight trade run is survivable and boring. If it is not, you have not built a strategy, you have built a countdown. The probability framing here is the same one in trading is a game of probability.

The rule that can end the question for you

There is a scenario where the duration question becomes irrelevant, because the position is closed for you. Under the product intervention measures adopted by the European Securities and Markets Authority for contracts for difference, firms must close a retail client's position when margin falls to 50 per cent of the minimum required margin, and leverage for retail clients is capped by asset class, at 30 to 1 for major currency pairs down to 2 to 1 for cryptocurrencies, with negative balance protection applied per account. The detail is in ESMA's final product intervention measures on CFDs and binary options.

The reader who wrote that his positions never touched the stop loss but still hit a maximum drawdown limit had run into exactly this class of rule. Floating losses across several open positions can breach an account level limit even when no individual stop is reached. A close out rule does not care about your recovery plan. It acts on the account balance, which means depth is not only expensive, it is also the thing that can remove your ability to continue at all. What a margin call is and how to avoid one covers that mechanism properly.

What actually shortens a drawdown

Three things shorten it, and two popular ones lengthen it.

What helps, first, is stopping the digging. Every additional loss increases the required gain faster than it increased the loss, so halting the decline is worth more than any recovery trade. Second, reducing size while you are in the hole. This feels backwards because smaller size means slower recovery, but it also means the floor stops falling, and a slow climb from a fixed floor beats a fast climb from a floor that keeps dropping. Third, time and consistency, which is the unglamorous answer the arithmetic keeps producing.

What lengthens it is raising risk to recover faster. Double your risk per trade and you halve the number of losses needed to deepen the hole. The reader who described letting a position run because he did not want to accept the loss, and losing again and again, was running that experiment in real time. Also lengthening it: trading more often to find the recovery, which multiplies cost and exposure at precisely the moment the account can least afford either.

Frequently asked questions

How long will my drawdown last if I keep trading the same way?

At best, the time it takes to earn the required gain at your sustainable monthly pace, and in practice longer, because the smooth-pace calculation assumes no further losses. A 20 per cent drawdown needs a 25.00 per cent gain, which at a net one per cent a month is about 22.4 months before any setback is counted. If the way you have been trading is what produced the drawdown, the realistic answer is longer still.

Is a drawdown the same as a loss?

No. A loss is a single outcome. A drawdown is the distance from your highest account value to the current one, so it accumulates across trades and it persists until a new high is made. You can have a profitable month and still be in drawdown.

Why does a 50 per cent drawdown need a 100 per cent gain?

Because the gain is earned on what is left, not on what you had. Halving 100 leaves 50, and getting 50 back to 100 is a doubling. The percentage that took it down and the percentage needed to bring it back are measured against different balances, which is the whole asymmetry.

Should I reduce my position size while in drawdown?

Reducing size slows the climb but it also stops the floor falling, and the arithmetic says depth is far more expensive than time. Many traders do the opposite and raise size to recover faster, which shortens the distance to a far deeper hole. This is education rather than advice on your specific account, and the right size depends on circumstances only you can see.

How many losses in a row is normal?

More than people expect. Across a hundred trades that lose half the time, a streak of five is about 81.0 per cent likely and a streak of eight about 17.0 per cent likely. If the approach loses 60 per cent of the time, an eight loss streak is close to a coin flip. Normal runs look like broken strategies to anyone who has not done the counting.

Can a drawdown end without me doing anything differently?

Yes, if the approach genuinely has an edge and the drawdown was ordinary variance. That is precisely why the depth limit matters: it keeps you solvent long enough for an ordinary bad run to pass. The problem is that you cannot tell from inside the drawdown whether it is variance or deterioration, so survival rules have to work in both cases.

Where this leaves you

The useful answer to how long will my drawdown last is that the depth decides the size of the climb, your sustainable pace decides the speed, and your risk per trade decides whether the hole stops getting deeper while you climb. Nobody can give you a date, but anyone can give you the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says something clear: protect the depth. A 10 per cent hole is an 11.11 per cent climb and a nuisance. A 50 per cent hole is a doubling and a different life.

So the practical move is not to find a faster recovery. It is to put a floor under the decline, make the size small enough that an ordinary eight loss run is boring, and let a modest pace do work that a heroic one cannot. The traders who come back are rarely the ones who recovered quickly. They are the ones who never let it get deep enough to need a miracle.
